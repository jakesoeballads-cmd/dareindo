The problem

Abandoned land on slopes has lost the plants that once held its soil together. In heavy rain, slopes like these are the most likely to slide.

Often this is not because people do not care. Land is switched to seasonal crops because families need to eat now, while the benefits of a forest take years to arrive.

A layered forest, not a bamboo plantation

Bamboo cannot hold a slope on its own. Its fibrous roots sit mostly in the topsoil, so it is good at stopping surface erosion but does not anchor the deeper layers where large landslides start.

That is why Akar Bambu plants three layers: deep-rooted native trees to anchor the slope, local clumping bamboo to hold the surface and stream banks, and income crops in the shade. The land recovers first; only then is part of it harvested.

What we will plant

  • Deep-rooted native treesThey anchor the slope and give local wildlife a home. Species are chosen per site with experts, from local seedlings, and are never felled.
  • Local clumping bambooSuch as bambu apus or bambu betung, which grow in clumps. We do not use running bamboo species, which can become invasive.
  • Income cropsChosen with local people after a market study, to suit local soil and climate. Katuk and vanilla are candidates, not decisions.
  • Protected zonesThe steepest parts of the slope and stream banks are planted for protection only, with no harvesting and no haul paths.

The species list, the ratio of native trees to income crops, the protected zones and the harvesting rules go into a technical plan for each site, drawn up with forestry experts and published before planting.

Starting with one pilot site

We start with one small site of about 1 to 5 hectares, chosen deliberately. One site that succeeds and can be checked is better than many that are neglected.

  • Clear land statusOwnership or management rights are recorded in writing before any work begins.
  • With existing groupsA forest farmers’ group, village or local community is a partner, not just a beneficiary.
  • Consent without pressureResidents and landowners get full information first, including the risks, and are free to say no.
  • State forest and customary landOnly through the Social Forestry scheme or with the consent of the customary community concerned.

Land reports sent through this site are still recorded as candidates for later sites.

Local people and benefit sharing

Local people know the land best, so they help decide which crops to plant, the harvesting rules and how work is shared on site.

Local workers are paid to plant, tend and harvest under a written employment agreement, at no less than the local minimum wage.

Before planting, we agree a written benefit-sharing agreement that sets out the shares of the landowner, workers, the community group and Daya Reforestasi Indonesia. We will publish an anonymised version on the Transparency page.

Wages and all money flows are recorded in the Public Cash Book by role, not by name, so anyone can check how much reaches the field without exposing anyone’s personal data.

From site to harvest

  1. Choose a siteWith a village or farmers’ group, checking the land status.
  2. ConsultationResidents and landowners agree the plan and the benefit-sharing agreement.
  3. BaselineSoil condition, erosion and household income before the project.
  4. Technical planSpecies, protected zones and harvesting rules, drawn up with experts.
  5. Plant and tendDone by paid local workers.
  6. Measure and harvestResults are measured, then harvested under the agreed rules.

How we measure success

The number of seedlings planted is not enough. We will report these four measures at month 6 and month 12, compared with the baseline.

  • Trees still aliveThe share of seedlings alive after 12 months, not just how many were planted.
  • Land coverThe area covered by canopy and soil-holding plants.
  • Erosion and small landslidesSigns of erosion and small landslides on site.
  • Local incomeThe change in participating households’ income compared with before the project.
